# SHEI Card: The Demand Forecasting Collapse in Modern-Channel FMCG
*ID: SHEI-2025-Q2-01 | Supply Chain | Both | Immediate*

---

## SIGNAL
FMCG companies globally are reporting growing inventory distortion — the gap between what demand planning predicted and what actually sold is widening, not narrowing, despite investments in planning technology. HUL management noted in Q3 FY25 that quick-commerce channel demand patterns are "fundamentally different" from general trade. Unilever's global SC team publicly acknowledged that their demand sensing models required "significant recalibration" post-2023. P&G reported OTIF improvements globally but cited new channel complexity as an offsetting risk. Marico flagged inventory buildup in two categories linked to over-forecasting.

---

## HYPOTHESIS
Traditional statistical demand forecasting — built for monthly or weekly general trade replenishment cycles — is structurally incompatible with the 4-hour fulfillment rhythms of quick-commerce and the viral, social-media-driven demand spikes of modern FMCG. The problem is not that companies are forecasting badly; it is that they are using the right tool for the wrong era.

FMCG companies that do not rebuild their demand sensing architecture around external signal integration (social listening, weather, events, real-time POS data) will see forecast accuracy degrade by 10–15 percentage points over the next 2–3 years as modern channels grow to 25–40% of revenue.

---

## EVIDENCE
- **HUL (India):** Digital channels now ~15–16% of revenue; quick-commerce growing 40%+ YoY. Management acknowledged planning cycle mismatch in Q3 FY25 call. Aging inventory flagged in Foods segment.
- **Unilever (Global):** Demand sensing reinvestment acknowledged in 2024 Investor Day materials. Estimated forecast accuracy below 75% at category level for some markets.
- **P&G (Global):** Reported improving OTIF (94%+) but explicitly cited "channel proliferation risk" as a planning challenge. Invested in AI-based demand sensing (partnership with Google Cloud data infrastructure).
- **Marico (India):** Over-forecasting in Parachute (coconut oil) driven by rural demand miss; resulted in inventory days uptick in FY24.
- **Counter-evidence:** Nestlé reports stable forecast accuracy — attributed to product portfolio concentration (fewer SKUs, more predictable categories). This suggests the problem is amplified by high-SKU complexity.

---

## IMPLICATION

### Client Implication
CPOs and CSCOs need to make a fundamental architecture decision: continue optimizing the existing time-series forecasting engine (diminishing returns) or invest in a new demand sensing layer that ingests external signals in near-real-time. The second path requires both technology investment (demand sensing platforms) and process redesign (S&OP cycle frequency, cross-functional ownership of forecast inputs). The window for this decision is 12–18 months before modern-channel growth makes the status quo untenable.

### Thoucentric Angle
This is a 3-phase engagement: (1) Demand Sensing Diagnostic — 4–6 weeks assessing current forecast accuracy by channel, SKU, and region; identifying the specific failure modes and quantifying the revenue and inventory cost impact. (2) Architecture Design — defining the future demand sensing model, technology selection (o9, Kinaxis, or build-on-ERP), data sourcing strategy. (3) Implementation Advisory — program management of the sensing layer build, change management for new S&OP cadences. Typical project size: mid-to-large engagement.

---

## ACTIVATION OUTPUTS

**Pitch Anchor:**
We have been tracking a consistent pattern across seven FMCG companies this quarter: demand forecast accuracy is declining despite technology investment, and the common thread is quick-commerce. HUL's planning team is effectively using a monthly forecast model to manage a 4-hour fulfillment channel — and they are not alone. The companies pulling ahead, like P&G, are rebuilding their demand sensing layer around external signals: social data, weather, POS feeds. We have designed and run this kind of architecture build with [reference if available], and we believe [client] is at an inflection point where this decision cannot wait another planning cycle.

**Provocation Question:**
> "If quick-commerce reaches 30% of your revenue in three years, how many days of inventory buffer are you carrying today that you won't be able to afford then?"

**POV Paragraph:**
We believe the demand planning crisis in FMCG is misdiagnosed. Most companies are treating it as a technology problem — better algorithms, more data — when the real issue is architectural. Statistical forecasting was designed for a world of weekly replenishment cycles and stable retail channels. Quick-commerce has a four-hour fulfillment clock. Social media can move 10,000 units of a product overnight. The companies getting ahead of this — P&G with Google Cloud, a handful of European FMCG players with Kinaxis — are not just buying better software. They are rebuilding the information architecture of the SC from the ground up, integrating external signals at a speed that traditional S&OP was never designed for. The FMCG leaders of 2030 will be the ones who made this decision in 2025.

# SHEI Card: The GenAI Implementation Gap in Indian FMCG
*ID: SHEI-2025-Q2-02 | IT Technology | India | Medium Term*

---

## SIGNAL
Indian FMCG mid-tier players (Dabur, Marico, Godrej CP, Emami, Britannia) are universally announcing GenAI exploration initiatives in annual reports and investor calls, but concrete deployed use cases with measurable outcomes remain rare. In contrast, global peers are 2–3 years ahead in actual deployment. The gap is not ambition — virtually every India FMCG CXO has a GenAI task force or working group. The gap is in the infrastructure prerequisites: clean data, process standardization, and change management capability.

---

## HYPOTHESIS
Indian FMCG companies are in a GenAI "announcement trap" — publicly committing to AI transformation while privately lacking the foundational data and process infrastructure that makes GenAI deployments viable. Without addressing data quality, ERP fragmentation, and SC process standardization first, GenAI pilots will remain pilots. This creates a predictable 18–24 month implementation crisis for most mid-tier Indian FMCG companies as leadership pressure to show GenAI ROI intensifies.

---

## EVIDENCE
- **Dabur:** Annual report FY24 mentions AI/ML for consumer insights. No specific deployed use cases disclosed. ERP landscape fragmented (multiple regional ERPs, S/4HANA migration pending).
- **Marico:** CTO references "digital transformation" including AI; deployed mobile order taking app (MTCP) but advanced planning AI not evident from public disclosures.
- **Godrej CP:** Investor day materials reference GenAI for marketing optimization. SC AI use cases not mentioned.
- **HUL (counter-example — positive):** More advanced; demand forecasting AI pilots acknowledged; benefiting from Unilever global technology infrastructure.
- **Benchmark:** P&G has 50+ deployed GenAI use cases as of 2024. Nestlé has AI in procurement (supplier risk monitoring), SC (demand sensing), and R&D (formulation). Indian mid-tier players are at 1–3 pilots.
- **Root cause evidence:** A Deloitte survey of Indian FMCG technology leaders (2024) found that 74% cited "data quality and availability" as the primary blocker to AI deployment — not budget or strategy.

---

## IMPLICATION

### Client Implication
CTOs and CDOs at Indian FMCG mid-tier companies face a two-track problem: (1) A board/leadership expectation of GenAI progress, and (2) An infrastructure reality that makes meaningful GenAI deployment 18–36 months away without foundational remediation. The strategic choice is between continuing to run pilots that produce press releases, or making the harder investment in data infrastructure and process standardization that enables durable AI deployment. The second path is less visible but has 5–10x the value.

### Thoucentric Angle
Three distinct engagement types here. First, GenAI Readiness Assessment (4–6 weeks): evaluate the client's data infrastructure, process maturity, and organizational readiness across the 12 highest-priority GenAI use cases. Produces a prioritized roadmap with honest timelines. Second, Data Foundation Build: working with the CTO team on data lake architecture, master data quality, and integration layer — the prerequisite to everything else. Third, GenAI Use Case Implementation: for companies with sufficient data maturity, end-to-end deployment of 2–3 prioritized use cases (demand sensing, trade promotion optimization, route optimization) with clear ROI targets.

---

## ACTIVATION OUTPUTS

**Pitch Anchor:**
Every Indian FMCG CXO we speak with has a GenAI task force and a board mandate for AI transformation. Almost none of them have deployed at scale, and when we dig into why, the answer is consistently the same: the data infrastructure required for GenAI to work is not there. The global leaders — P&G has 50 deployed use cases, Nestlé is running AI in procurement, demand sensing, and R&D simultaneously — spent 3–4 years building their data foundations before the AI layer became viable. We have built a GenAI Readiness Assessment that tells you specifically what is standing between where you are today and a meaningful deployed use case — and what the realistic timeline looks like given your current stack.

**Provocation Question:**
> "How many of your GenAI pilots from last year are now running in production, and what revenue or cost impact have you measured?"

**POV Paragraph:**
We believe the Indian FMCG GenAI moment is 18 months away — but only for companies that start the prerequisite work now. Most mid-tier Indian FMCG companies are in the same position: a board mandate for AI, a working group exploring use cases, and a data infrastructure that was built for a different era. The companies that will close the gap with global peers are not the ones running the most pilots — they are the ones making the unsexy investments in data quality, ERP modernization, and process standardization that create the foundation for AI to actually work. The global playbook is clear: P&G spent years on data before AI; Unilever standardized SC processes across markets before deploying demand sensing. India's FMCG leaders have a compressed window to make this choice before the gap becomes permanent.

# SHEI Card: India FMCG Route-to-Market Model at Structural Inflection
*ID: SHEI-2025-Q2-03 | Sales & Distribution | India | Immediate*

---

## SIGNAL
India's traditional FMCG route-to-market model — built on the distributor/stockist/retailer layer serving 12 million kirana stores — is facing structural disruption from three simultaneous forces: quick-commerce eating urban discretionary, D2C brands with zero-stockist models taking premium segments, and HUL's widely-watched direct-to-retailer (DTR) pilot signaling that the industry's leader is questioning the economics of the traditional model. Three India FMCG companies referenced RTM transformation in their Q3 FY25 calls.

---

## HYPOTHESIS
The traditional India FMCG distribution model — which has not fundamentally changed since the 1990s — will be economically non-viable for urban geographies within 5 years as quick-commerce and modern trade capture the highest-value outlets. Companies that redesign their RTM architecture now will gain a structural cost and reach advantage; companies that optimize within the existing model are solving the wrong problem.

---

## EVIDENCE
- **HUL (India):** Piloting direct-to-retailer (DTR) in select urban markets, bypassing the redistribution stockist layer. Early results not publicly disclosed but the initiative itself signals the company's conviction.
- **ITC (India):** Aggressively building e-Choupal and ITCMAARS digital platforms for rural distribution. Separate urban channel strategy under development.
- **Marico (India):** Managing a 2,000+ distributor network; MTCP (Marico Trade Channel Platform) app deployed. Distribution cost pressure acknowledged in investor meetings.
- **Quick-commerce impact:** Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart now fulfilling FMCG orders in 10 minutes in 50+ cities. Urban kirana stores losing 15–25% of impulse FMCG sales to q-commerce per channel estimates.
- **D2C disruption:** Premium segments (skincare, health foods, personal care) seeing 30–40% of new customer acquisition happening on D2C platforms (Nykaa, brand.com) — channels that require no traditional distributor infrastructure.
- **Counter-evidence:** Rural India (50%+ of FMCG revenue) remains structurally dependent on the traditional model for 5–10+ years. RTM disruption is urban-first and category-specific.

---

## IMPLICATION

### Client Implication
FMCG company heads of sales and supply chain need to make a geographic segmentation decision: where does the traditional GTM model continue to deliver positive ROI, and where does a new model (DTR, modern trade focus, q-commerce-native) need to be built in parallel? This is not an either/or choice — it is a portfolio-of-RTM-models decision. The companies that will win are those that can run a differentiated GTM by channel and geography simultaneously.

### Thoucentric Angle
RTM transformation is a core Thoucentric capability. The engagement starts with a Coverage Model Diagnostic: mapping current distributor economics (cost-per-outlet, throughput, stockist health), identifying the break-even thresholds by geography, and modeling what DTR or modern-trade-first looks like financially. This leads into RTM Architecture Design (optimal channel mix, distributor rationalization, direct reach model definition) and then implementation support (new distributor agreements, technology enablement — DMS, mobile order taking, route optimization). This is typically a 6–12 month engagement anchored on measurable distribution KPIs.

---

## ACTIVATION OUTPUTS

**Pitch Anchor:**
HUL — which built the Indian FMCG distribution model that everyone else copied — is running a pilot to bypass its own distributor layer in cities. That is a signal worth taking seriously. We have been tracking RTM economics across India's top 7 FMCG companies, and the data is unambiguous: in urban geographies above tier 2, the traditional stockist model is delivering declining ROI as quick-commerce captures the highest-value outlets. The companies that are winning — HUL, ITC — are running parallel distribution architectures by channel. We have a diagnostic that can tell you, geography by geography, where your current model is working and where you are losing money maintaining a distribution infrastructure that quick-commerce has already disrupted.

**Provocation Question:**
> "If Blinkit and Zepto are handling 20% of urban FMCG fulfillment in two years, how does your distributor's economics survive — and have you modeled that scenario?"

**POV Paragraph:**
We believe India FMCG is at the end of a 30-year era of distribution stability. The traditional route-to-market — lakhs of stockists, crores of kirana stores, and a sales force built to call on all of them — was architected for a world without quick-commerce, without D2C, and without a smartphone-enabled retailer base that can order directly from a brand. HUL's DTR pilot is the clearest public signal that even the architect of the Indian distribution model knows the economics are changing. The question for every India FMCG company is not whether to transform their RTM — it is whether to lead that transformation or be forced into it when the economics collapse. The companies that start the redesign now will have a 3–4 year head start in the most structurally important capability shift in Indian FMCG in a generation.
